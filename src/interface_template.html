Research tool, not investment advice. Rankings and estimated returns come from a GARCH-filtered Markov simulation backtested across each stock's recent ex-dividend events, not a prediction of future performance. It can model a stock's own historical calendar pattern around its dividend date, but it cannot predict a one-off news- or macro-driven price move — see the TTE example in AI_Performance_Report.md, which the "hit rate" and "± std dev" figures below are meant to keep visible rather than hidden behind a single confident-looking number.
📖 New here? Read this first — what everything on this page means

What this page does: dividend-capture is a strategy where you buy a stock shortly before it pays a dividend, collect the payout, then sell. The catch is that stock prices usually drop by about the dividend amount the moment it's paid — so the trick is timing your buy and sell so the price has room to recover before/after that drop. This page ran a computer simulation on each stock's real price history to estimate good timing, and checked how well that timing would actually have worked across that stock's past dividend payouts (not just one, several — see "hit rate" below).

Buy — "N days before ex-div"
The estimated best day to purchase, counted backward from the dividend date. "3 days before" means: if the dividend date is a Friday, buy on the preceding Tuesday.
Sell — "N days after ex-div"
The estimated day the price should have recovered enough (price gain + dividend received) to come out ahead, counted forward from the dividend date. "No recovery within __POST_WINDOW__d" means the simulation didn't find a breakeven point within __POST_WINDOW__ trading days — a red flag, not a typo.
Hit rate (with a confidence range)
Of this stock's last several dividend payouts, what fraction actually recovered in time when we replay the same buy/sell rule on each one. 100% is rare and not a guarantee — it means "N for N last time," not "will always work." The range in brackets (e.g. "90% [76–96%]") is a statistical confidence interval: with a small number of past events, the true underlying rate could plausibly be anywhere in that range — a narrow range means the number is well-supported, a wide one means treat the headline percentage with real caution.
Return per cycle (avg ± std dev)
The average profit or loss (as a % of what you put in) across those past cycles, and how much that result actually varied between them. A small ± number means the outcome was fairly consistent each time; a large ± number means it sometimes did much better or much worse than the average — treat the average as a rough middle, not a promise.
Risk-reward score
One number combining average return, how many days your money is tied up, and hit rate — but using the low end of the hit rate's confidence range, not the headline percentage, so a stock that looks great only because it's had very few events doesn't automatically outrank one with a slightly lower but much better-supported hit rate. Used only to rank stocks against each other; higher is better, but the number alone means nothing outside this comparison.
"Long-history model" vs "Recent-movement model" (buttons above)
Two ways the computer estimated future prices: one leans on this stock's entire price history for a steadier, less reactive estimate; the other leans only on the last year or so, so it reacts faster to recent momentum but can be noisier. Neither is "correct" — compare both if a stock's numbers look very different between them, that's a sign the estimate is less certain.
"Conservative pick" / "Aggressive pick" / "Balanced" / "Not recommended" (Quick picks table below)
Not the same thing as the model buttons above — this is about your risk appetite, not the computer's math. Conservative pick = historically the most reliable, smaller swings. Aggressive pick = historically the biggest average payoff, but less consistent. Balanced = middling on both. Not recommended = weak track record on reliability, average return, or both.

"Auto-refreshing in 60s" (top right of the header)
This page reloads itself from disk every 60 seconds — it does not fetch live market data or re-run the analysis by itself. The numbers only change after the next reload if something else (rerunning analysis_engine.py, by hand or on a schedule) has actually regenerated this file in the meantime. Check the "Generated" timestamp at the top — if it hasn't moved, you're looking at the same snapshot, just re-loaded.
